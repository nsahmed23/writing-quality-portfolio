Short version: it almost certainly doesn't. A different brand of flour does not kill a starter. Mature starters are dense colonies of wild yeast and lactobacilli, and they survive flour changes routinely. A new flour changes how *fast* and how *strongly* fermentation goes, and the switch happened in the same week as something else that actually slowed you down.

I can't see your starter or your kitchen, so I can't confirm which cause is yours. Here is how to find out.

**First, separate two failure modes.** They are unrelated problems:

1. The starter itself isn't bubbling or doubling in the jar.
2. The starter looks fine, but the dough or loaf doesn't rise.

**Test which one you have (about 8 hours).** Feed 20g starter + 40g flour + 40g water by weight, mark the level on the jar with a rubber band, and keep it around 75 to 80F. A healthy starter at least doubles and domes within 4 to 8 hours. If it does that, the starter is fine and you have problem 2.

**What the flour change really did.** Each of these slows fermentation or changes dough feel; none of them sterilizes anything.

- **Protein.** Bread flour absorbs more water than all-purpose. If you kept your water weight the same, the dough is now stiffer and rises less visibly. Add 3 to 5% more water (15 to 25g per 500g flour).
- **Malted vs unmalted.** Most US all-purpose contains malted barley flour, which supplies enzymes that free up sugar for the yeast. Unmalted flour ferments slower.
- **Whole grain vs white.** Whole wheat and rye ferment faster; straight white flour is slower.
- **Bleached flour.** Mildly slower, not fatal. Use unbleached if it's an option.
- **Adaptation.** A starter takes a few days to hit full speed on a new flour, then recovers on its own.

**The confounder I'd bet on: temperature.** As a rough rule of thumb (not a precise curve), fermentation roughly halves in pace for every 15 to 20F drop. It's September. If your kitchen went from 78F to 68F, a dough that peaked in 5 hours now needs 9 or 10, and a starter that doubled overnight now looks dead in the morning. That lines up with a flour switch by coincidence, not cause.

**Fix path.** Feed twice a day at that 1:2:2 ratio for two or three days somewhere warm (oven with just the light on, or on top of the fridge). If it's still sluggish, make 10 to 20% of the flour in each feed whole rye; rye is the reliable jump start. Then proof by rise, not by clock: wait for the dough to grow 50 to 75% and feel airy, however long that takes.

**Actually dead is rare.** Fuzzy mold or pink and orange streaks mean throw it out. Grey liquid on top (hooch) and a nail-polish smell mean hungry, not dead: pour it off and feed.
