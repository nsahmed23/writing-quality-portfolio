The new flour almost certainly didn't kill your starter. A mature starter is a dense mixed culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria living in an acidic environment that fends off most competitors, and ordinary retail flour is neither sterile nor antimicrobial. What a flour change reliably does is change how *fast* the culture ferments and how well the dough holds the gas it produces. From outside the jar those look like the same failure, and they have opposite fixes, so that's where I'd start.

**First, split the symptom**

"Stopped rising" means one of two things, and they are not the same problem:

1. The starter itself isn't rising in its jar: no reliable doubling, few bubbles, smell gone flat or sharply solvent-like.
2. The starter looks normal but the loaf bakes flat and dense.

If it's the second one, the culture is fine and no amount of starter fussing will help.

**How a flour swap actually changes things (slower, not dead)**

Bran and germ are the big one. Whole grain and higher-extraction flours carry minerals, more enzyme activity, and more wild microbes. Moving to a whiter, more refined flour takes food away and slows fermentation noticeably. This is the single most common cause behind "my starter got lazy after I switched brands."

Malted versus unmalted matters almost as much. Most US all-purpose and bread flour has malted barley flour added, which supplies amylase to break starch into sugar the yeast can eat. Many Canadian and European flours are unmalted. Switching from malted to unmalted removes the ready-made sugar supply, and things get slower and less vigorous. Bleached flour gets blamed here too; it does behave differently in the dough, but it doesn't sterilize anything.

Protein and water absorption are the flat-loaf half. Bread flour runs about 12 to 13.5% protein, all-purpose about 10 to 11.5%. At the same water weight, the lower-protein flour makes a slacker dough that ferments perfectly well and then spreads sideways instead of up. That reads as "didn't rise" and it's a structure problem, not a yeast problem.

One thing cuts against the flour theory: a starter normally re-equilibrates to a new flour within two or three feeding cycles. You're a week in, so pure flour adaptation is already a weak explanation on its own.

**Stronger suspects**

Temperature trips up more people than flour ever does. As a rule of thumb in the normal kitchen range, fermentation rate roughly halves for every 10 C (18 F) drop, so a kitchen that slid from 24 C to 19 C makes everything take about twice as long. Keep your old timings and you're just cutting the dough short. Worth asking what else changed the same week: the season, the heating, where the jar sits.

Real culture-killers, for completeness: water above roughly 50 C (120 F) at feeding time, detergent residue in a freshly scrubbed jar, or a changed feeding ratio that starves or over-dilutes it. Chlorinated tap water is blamed constantly and is almost never the cause with an established starter. Also consider that "stopped rising" sometimes means "rose and collapsed before I looked," which is over-proofing, the opposite problem.

**What to actually do**

1. Run a side-by-side feed. Two straight-sided jars, each 20 g starter + 20 g flour + 20 g water, one with the new flour and one with the old bag (whole wheat or rye if the old bag is gone). Same water, same spot, rubber band at the starting line. A healthy starter roughly doubles in 4 to 8 hours at 24 to 26 C.
2. Measure the real temperature with a probe in the starter or dough, not the room thermostat. Below about 21 C, that alone explains a week of flat loaves.
3. If both jars double, the culture is fine, so fix the dough. Check the protein on the new bag: lower protein, cut water by 3 to 5% and shorten the bulk ferment; more whole grain or higher protein, add 3 to 5% water.
4. If only the old flour doubles, the new flour genuinely is slower. Don't fight it, feed with 10 to 20% whole wheat or rye mixed in. That restores the mineral and enzyme supply and usually fixes the timing within two feeds.
5. Judge nothing on one cycle. Give it three feeds; a single observation on a mixed culture is noise.
