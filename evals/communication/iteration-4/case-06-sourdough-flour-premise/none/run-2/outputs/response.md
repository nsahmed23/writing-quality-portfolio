The new flour almost certainly did not kill your starter, and your starter is probably not dead. Ordinary wheat flour contains nothing that kills yeast or lactic acid bacteria, and a mature starter is an acidic, crowded, competitive culture that shrugs off nearly anything you feed it. What a brand switch genuinely can do is slow fermentation enough that it looks dead. That is the far more likely story, and it usually resolves within a few feedings.

**How to rule out actual death first.** A starter is really gone in only a few situations, and all of them are visible. Fuzzy mold of any color, or pink and orange streaks: discard the whole jar, do not scrape. Cooked above roughly 120 F (50 C), which happens in an oven with the light on or on a summer windowsill: dead. Anything else is alive. Dark liquid on top (hooch) and a nail-polish-remover smell mean starving, not dead; starters neglected in a fridge for months routinely come back.

**What a flour swap actually changes,** roughly in order of how often it explains this:

- **Malt.** Check both bags for "malted barley flour" in the ingredients. Most US supermarket bread and all-purpose flours have it; many organic, imported, and stone-ground flours do not. Malt supplies amylase, the enzyme that breaks starch into sugars the yeast can eat. Remove it and every feed starts slower and peaks later. This is the single most common cause of "my starter died when I changed brands."
- **Whole grain fraction.** If the old bag was whole wheat, or you were topping white flour with a little rye, and the new bag is straight white, you cut the mineral and enzyme supply hard. Bran and germ carry most of the fermentation fuel.
- **Protein and water absorption.** A 12.5% protein bread flour absorbs noticeably more water than a 10.5% all-purpose. Same recipe, same water weight, new flour, and the dough is slacker, holds gas worse, and spreads sideways instead of rising up. The culture is fine; the dough cannot hold what it produces. Going the other direction gives you a tight dough that rises slowly and less visibly.
- **Bleached vs unbleached.** Bakers widely report bleached all-purpose ferments sluggishly. The evidence is anecdotal rather than well established, so treat this as a suspect, not a verdict. (If bromate is the worry: it is a dough oxidizer for gluten strength, not an antimicrobial.)

**Something else probably changed last week too.** Room temperature is the usual hidden variable. Fermentation speed roughly doubles per 10 C (18 F) in the normal kitchen range, so a drop from 76 F to 65 F adds about half again to the time to peak with nothing else different. If your water source changed as well, note that chloramine in municipal water does not off-gas the way chlorine does, so letting water sit out overnight will not strip it.

**The test that settles it.** Split the starter into two jars. Feed both identically, same ratio, same water, same shelf, one with the old flour and one with the new. Mark the starting level with a rubber band and record hours to peak, meaning the highest point before it starts sinking back, rather than whether it doubles by some fixed time. Run it twice. If both jars are sluggish, the problem is the starter or the environment, not the flour. If the old flour peaks clearly faster, it is the flour, and it is a nutrient and adaptation issue rather than death. Skip the float test entirely; whether a blob floats depends on hydration and how much you stirred, so it produces false alarms on perfectly healthy starters. If the old flour is gone, run the same test with new flour in both jars, one plain and one with 20% whole rye mixed in. A rye jar that takes off confirms the nutrient explanation.

**The fix, which works regardless of which cause it turns out to be.** Feed 1:1:1 by weight (equal weights of starter, water, and flour) twice a day for two or three days, replacing 10% to 25% of the feeding flour with whole rye or whole wheat. Hold it at 75 to 80 F: an oven with the light off and the door cracked, the top of the fridge, a cupboard near a warm appliance. Use filtered or bottled water if your tap smells of chlorine. Once it peaks reliably in four to eight hours, go back to baking and re-dial hydration for the new flour, holding back 20 to 30 g of water per 500 g of flour if you moved to a lower-protein flour and adding that much if you moved up.
