Your starter is almost certainly not dead, and the new flour almost certainly did not kill it. Plain flour is not sterile and is not toxic to yeast; a mature starter is a dense, acidic culture that outcompetes nearly anything you feed it. What a flour change reliably does is move the *timing* and the *height* of the rise, and a starter whose peak has shifted from hour 5 to hour 11 looks exactly like a dead starter if you keep checking at hour 5.

Also worth separating: the switch and the failure landed in the same week, but "after" is not "because." Last week probably brought other changes too, and one of them (temperature) is the most common cause of a starter that suddenly quits.

**First, which thing stopped rising?**

These are two different problems with different fixes:

- The starter in the jar doesn't grow after a feed. That is a fermentation problem: microbes, food, temperature.
- The starter rises fine in the jar but the dough sits flat. That is a gas retention problem: gluten strength, shaping, proof time. Flour changes cause this one far more often, and it says nothing about your starter's health.

**What a brand switch actually changes**

- Whole grain fraction. Whole wheat and rye carry more minerals, more enzymes, and more of their own microbes; white flour has almost none. Going from any whole-grain content to straight white flour can double or triple the time to peak. This is the most common genuine flour effect.
- Malted vs unmalted. Many US all-purpose and bread flours list "malted barley flour" in the ingredients. That malt supplies amylase, an enzyme that breaks starch into sugars the yeast can eat. An unmalted flour ferments noticeably slower, especially for the first few feeds.
- Protein content. Check the nutrition panel: about 3 g protein per 30 g serving is roughly 10% protein (all-purpose), 4 g is roughly 13% (bread flour). Lower protein means weaker gluten, so dough spreads instead of doming. The microbes don't care; the loaf does.
- Water absorption. Different flours take up different amounts of water, so your usual 1:1:1 feed by weight lands at a different consistency. A looser starter rises less and collapses faster, which makes the peak easy to miss entirely.
- Things on the bag worth ruling out: "self-rising" (contains salt and baking powder, and you want neither), "bleached," and "heat-treated" or "safe to eat raw." None of those are instant death, but bleached and heat-treated flours are plausibly slower, and grabbing self-rising by mistake is common.

**What else changed last week**

Ambient temperature dominates everything above. Fermentation rate roughly doubles for every 18F (10C) of warming across the range a kitchen actually occupies; that rule breaks down at the extremes, and yeast activity falls off sharply above about 95F. In practice a drop from 78F to 68F stretches time to peak by something like 1.5 to 2 times, which is more than enough to make a healthy starter look dead on your old schedule. A moved jar, a new heating pattern, a bigger jar (rise is harder to see), a changed feeding ratio, or a longer gap between feeds all push the same direction.

Water gets blamed constantly and deserves it rarely. Municipal chlorine at tap levels slows an established starter at worst. Only consider it if you switched to something unusual like distilled or heavily softened water.

**Do this**

1. Look and smell first. Yeasty, tangy, sharp, or like acetone or nail polish remover means alive and hungry. A layer of dark liquid on top (hooch) means alive and very hungry. Pink or orange streaks, or fuzz of any color, means discard it and start over. Those are the only real death signs.
2. Rescue feed. Keep 20 g of starter, discard the rest, add 40 g flour and 40 g water at about 80F. Use the old flour if you still have some; otherwise unbleached all-purpose with about 20% whole wheat or rye stirred in. Mark the level on the jar with tape or a rubber band.
3. Keep it warm, 78 to 82F. An oven with only the light on, or the top of the fridge, usually gets there. Check at 4, 6, 8, and 12 hours rather than only the next morning; a missed peak is invisible.
4. Repeat twice a day for two or three days. Nearly every "dead" starter is doubling again within three feeds.
5. Then run the real test. Split it: feed one jar with the old flour and one with the new, same water, same temperature, same ratio, same jar shape, both marked. If they rise the same, the flour was never the variable. If the new one is clearly slower, you don't need the old brand back; blend in 20 to 25% rye or whole wheat permanently and give it more time.

Skip the float test. Whether a spoonful floats depends mostly on hydration and how much air you stirred in, so it produces confident wrong answers in both directions. A marked jar and a clock beat it.

**What I could not check**

I can't see your kitchen or your starter, so this is a ranked list of candidates, not a diagnosis. Unverified on my end: which brand you left and which you moved to, whether that bag is bleached, malted, whole grain, or self-rising, whether the jar or the loaf is what stopped rising, your kitchen temperature and feeding schedule, and how the starter looks and smells right now. Step 5 settles the flour question in about a day, and the rest you can rule in or out by reading the bag and putting a thermometer on the counter.
