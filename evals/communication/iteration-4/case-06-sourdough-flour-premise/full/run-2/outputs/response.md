Flour almost never kills a starter. The yeast and bacteria live in the culture itself and are carried forward in every feeding, so a new bag changes what they eat, not whether they survive. The things that genuinely kill a starter are heat above roughly 130 F (55 C), or contamination you can see: fuzzy growth, or pink and orange streaks. A different brand of wheat flour is not on that list.

What you have so far is a sequence, not a cause: the rise stopped after the switch. Two other things also change on a one-week timescale, your kitchen temperature and your dough, and both produce this exact symptom.

Run this before changing anything else. Fifteen minutes of hands-on work, then a wait of four to eight hours.

1. Feed two jars from the same starter: 20 g starter, 50 g flour, 50 g water in each. One gets the new flour. The other gets the old flour if that bag is still around; if not, use whole wheat or rye, which ferment fast and make a good reference point.
2. Mark the starting level on both jars with a rubber band or a strip of tape, put them in the same spot, and write down the room temperature.
3. Check hourly and record when each one peaks, meaning the moment the top stops doming and begins to flatten. At around 75 F (24 C) and that feeding ratio, a healthy starter usually peaks somewhere in the four to eight hour range.

If both jars peak at about the same time, your starter is fine and the flour is cleared; the problem is in the dough or the schedule. If the new-flour jar is much slower or barely moves, the flour is implicated, and the list below narrows which difference is doing it.

Four ways two bags of flour actually differ, ranked by what to check first:

1. **The label, on both bags.** Thirty seconds, and it settles two possibilities outright. Self-rising flour contains baking powder plus about 1.5% salt, and salt at that level slows fermentation badly while the chemical leavening ruins the crumb. Separately, most US white flour lists "malted barley flour" in the ingredients; that malt carries an enzyme that cuts starch into sugar the yeast can use immediately. Moving from a malted flour to an unmalted one, common with organic and imported brands, slows the early hours of the rise.
2. **Protein content.** Bread flour runs about 12 to 13 percent protein, all-purpose about 10 to 11, pastry about 8 to 9. Less protein means weaker gluten, so gas escapes instead of lifting the loaf. The tell is a lively starter paired with a dough that spreads sideways rather than up.
3. **Water absorption.** Whole grain and higher-protein flours take up more water. Keep the same recipe by weight and you get either a stiff dough that ferments slowly or a slack one that cannot hold gas. The tell is dough that feels different in the hand at the same hydration (water weight as a percentage of flour weight).
4. **Bleaching.** Bakers commonly report bleached all-purpose slowing their starters, and bleaching does alter the flour's starch and pH, but I have not seen a convincing demonstration that it slows an established culture. Last suspect, not first.

What I could not check: I cannot see your kitchen, your starter, the two bags, or how the dough felt. The competing explanation I would rank alongside the flour is temperature. Fermentation tracks it closely, and a kitchen a few degrees cooler than last week adds an hour or more to the same rise. If you did not measure room or dough temperature before and after the switch, a flour change and a temperature change look identical from the outside, which is precisely what the two-jar test separates.
