The new flour almost certainly did not kill your starter. A starter is a live culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria, and flour is what it eats, not a poison. Changing brands changes how fast it ferments and how the dough holds gas; it does not sterilize the jar. What you have is a slowdown that showed up at the same time as the new bag, and showing up at the same time is not the same as being the cause.

Worth separating two symptoms first, because they have different fixes: a jar that no longer bubbles and domes, versus a loaf that ferments but bakes flat and dense. Reason 3 below is usually the second one.

**What a flour swap genuinely changes, most likely first**

1. **Whole grain content.** Bran and germ carry minerals, enzymes, and their own resident microbes, so whole wheat and rye ferment noticeably faster than white flour. If the old bag was whole wheat, rye, or a higher extraction "artisan" flour and the new one is plain white, expect roughly half the visible activity at the same temperature. This is the most common version of your story.
2. **Malted barley flour.** Most US supermarket flour contains a little of it, which supplies amylase (an enzyme that cuts starch into sugars the yeast can actually eat). Many organic and imported European flours leave it out. Unmalted flour runs slower for the first several feeds while the culture adjusts.
3. **Protein content.** Bread flour is roughly 12 to 13 percent protein, all purpose roughly 10 to 11. Less protein means a weaker gluten network, so the dough can ferment perfectly well and still spread sideways instead of rising up. If the jar looks healthy and the loaf is the problem, start here; the starter is innocent.
4. **Water absorption.** Different flours drink different amounts of water, so the same recipe by weight now gives you a stiffer or slacker starter than before. A stiff one rises less visibly. A slack one can peak and fall back before you next look at it, which reads as "no rise." Check at hour 4 and hour 6, not only the next morning.
5. **Bleaching.** This is the only ingredient level candidate for real inhibition, and the support for it is scattered baker reports rather than anything solid. Even the pessimistic reading is "slower," not "dead." Only worth considering if the new bag says bleached.

**The variable you did not mention: temperature.** A kitchen that drifted from 24 °C to 19 °C (75 °F to 66 °F) roughly doubles the time to peak, and indoor temperatures start moving in September. A starter that used to peak at 6 hours and now needs 12 looks stalled if you keep checking on the old schedule.

**What actually kills a starter** is a short list: feed water much above 50 °C (120 °F), a heavy dose of salt, or visible fuzzy mold or pink and orange streaks (throw that one out). A dark liquid layer on top is hunger, not death. A sharp, nail-polish-remover smell is hunger too. Both mean feed it.

**The test that settles it**, if you still have any of the old flour:

1. Put 20 g of starter into each of two clean jars.
2. Feed one with 100 g old flour plus 100 g water, the other with 100 g new flour plus 100 g water, both at about 26 °C (79 °F).
3. Mark the starting level on each jar with a rubber band, and leave them side by side in the same spot.
4. Note the height at 4, 6, 8, and 12 hours.
5. Similar peaks clears the flour, and you are looking at temperature or schedule instead. A clear lag in the new flour jar makes the flour the variable, with reason 1 or 2 above as the mechanism.

I cannot see your starter, the two bags, or your kitchen temperature, so the ranking above is likelihood rather than a diagnosis of your jar. The side by side is what turns it into one.

Next, about a minute of work: feed the jar 20 g starter, 100 g flour with a quarter of that whole wheat or rye, and 100 g water at about 26 °C, put a rubber band at the level, and leave it somewhere warm. If the mark has moved by hour 6, nothing died and you have a speed problem, not a dead culture.
