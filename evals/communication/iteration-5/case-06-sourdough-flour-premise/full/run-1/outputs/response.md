The new flour is almost certainly not killing your starter. Flour is what the starter eats, and a mature culture has already lived through many bags; a brand change alters how fast the dough ferments and how well it holds gas, not whether the yeast and bacteria are alive. The rise stopped the week you switched, and nothing in that alone shows the flour did it. What makes this hard to see is that a sluggish starter, a weak dough, and a genuinely dead starter all produce the same flat loaf. I cannot see your starter, the two bags, or your kitchen, so everything below is ranked by what usually explains this, not by anything I have checked in your case.

## What the switch actually changed

- **Protein, which sets gluten strength.** Gluten is the elastic protein network that traps the gas fermentation produces. Bread flour runs about 12 to 14 percent protein, American all-purpose about 10 to 12, cake flour 7 to 9. Go from bread flour to a softer all-purpose and the dough still ferments, but the network is too weak to hold the gas, so the loaf spreads sideways instead of upward.
- **Water absorption.** Higher-protein and whole grain flours drink more water. If you kept your water weight the same, the same hydration (water weight as a percentage of flour weight) that gave a supple dough in the old flour can give a slack, flowing one in the new bag, or a stiff dough that cannot expand.
- **Bran, if the new bag is whole grain or stone milled.** Bran particles cut through the gluten strands and bring extra enzymes and minerals, which speeds fermentation up. Dough that used to peak in five hours may peak in three and already be collapsing when you bake it. Baking past the peak gives a dense loaf that looks like it never rose.
- **Malted or not.** Most large American white flours include malted barley flour, which supplies amylase, an enzyme that breaks starch down into sugars the culture can eat. Many organic and European flours are unmalted, so the same schedule frees less sugar and gives a slower, shallower rise.

## What genuinely harms a starter

- Water or dough hotter than about 50 C (120 F), which kills the yeast outright.
- Salt in the jar. A little slows it; repeated feeding with salted flour suppresses it.
- Weeks without feeding, which makes a starter acidic and slow rather than dead. Two or three daily feeds usually bring it back.
- Fuzzy mold, or pink and orange streaks. That is real spoilage; discard it and start again.

One brand change can do this: self-rising flour carries added salt and baking powder, and cake flour is both weak and chlorinated. If either is what you bought, the bag is the cause, and it hit the starter and the loaf at once.

## The other thing that changed last week

The flour changed and the kitchen temperature probably did too. Fermentation is very temperature sensitive: dough that peaks in five hours at 24 C (75 F) can need eight or more at 20 C (68 F). Keep the same clock through a cooler week and you bake underproofed dough with a starter that is in perfect health. The same goes for a new jar, a new proofing spot, or an old bag that had been open longer than you remembered.

## Telling these apart today

1. Read the new bag. Protein grams divided by serving grams gives the percentage: 4 g per 30 g serving is 13 percent, so bread flour; 3 g per 30 g is 10 percent, a soft all-purpose. Confirm it does not say self-rising, cake, or gluten free.
2. Feed 20 g of starter with 20 g flour and 20 g water, mark the starting level with tape or a rubber band, and leave it at 21 to 24 C (70 to 75 F). A healthy starter at least doubles, usually within 4 to 8 hours. The mark matters because a starter that rose and fell back while you were out looks identical to one that never moved. If it doubles, the culture is alive and the fault is in the dough or the timing.
3. If you still have the old flour, run two jars from the same starter side by side, one fed each flour, same weights, same shelf. That is the only test that answers the question you asked, and equal rises rule the flour out.

Where "flour is only food" stops holding: any wheat or rye flour will sustain a starter, but a white rice, corn, or tapioca blend gives it very little to work with and gives the loaf no gluten at all, and there the bag really is the whole story.
