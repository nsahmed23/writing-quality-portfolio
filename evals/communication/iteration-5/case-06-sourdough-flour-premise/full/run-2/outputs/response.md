A different brand of wheat flour does not kill a starter. Starters are hard to kill: the wild yeast and bacteria in them live on flour, and a new bag brings its own microbes rather than sterilizing anything. The loaf stopped rising the week you switched, but nothing in that yet shows the flour did it, and "the starter is dead" and "the loaf came out flat" are two different problems with different fixes.

### The two failures hiding behind "stopped rising"

Either the starter itself has gone quiet (it no longer roughly doubles in the jar after a feeding), or the starter is still strong and the dough is failing later, in the fermentation timing, the shaping, or the oven. Almost everything below depends on which one you have, so the jar test at the bottom comes first in practice.

### What a flour switch actually changes

1. **Protein.** Bread flour usually runs 12 to 14 percent protein, all-purpose 10 to 12, and brands vary inside those bands. Less protein, or weaker gluten (the protein network that traps the gas), means the dough spreads sideways instead of doming even when fermentation is perfect.
2. **Water absorption.** The same water weight makes a wetter dough in a flour that drinks less. A slack dough that pancakes on the peel looks identical to a dough that never rose.
3. **Speed.** Whole-grain or higher-extraction flour (more bran and germ left in) ferments faster, because it carries more nutrients and enzymes. On your old timings that dough overproofs: the gluten gives out before baking and the loaf sinks in the oven. This is one of the most common "it stopped rising" reports, and it is the opposite of under-fermented, so adding time makes it worse.
4. **Malt.** Many bread flours are malted, meaning they contain diastatic malt, an enzyme that breaks starch into sugar the yeast can eat. An unmalted flour starts slower for the first few hours.
5. **The label trap.** Check the bag says bread flour or all-purpose, not self-rising. Self-rising flour has added salt and baking powder, and the salt alone slows fermentation noticeably.

A starter fed unfamiliar flour also shifts rhythm for about three to five feedings before it settles. Slower, not dead.

### What does kill a starter

Sustained heat above roughly 50 C (120 F), weeks with no feeding, or visible fuzzy mold. A flour brand is not on that list.

### What else changed in the same week

House temperature is the usual culprit when a working routine stops working. Fermentation speed roughly halves for every 10 C (18 F) the dough cools, within normal kitchen range. A kitchen that drifted from 25 C to 19 C turns a five-hour bulk rise into ten-plus hours on exactly the same flour, which reads as a dead starter.

### The test that separates them

1. In a straight-sided jar, mix 25 g starter, 25 g of the new flour, and 25 g room-temperature water.
2. Mark the level with a rubber band or tape, leave it out, and write down the room temperature.
3. Check at 4, 8, and 12 hours. Doubling inside that window means the starter is healthy and the problem is downstream in the dough or the timing. Barely moving at 12 hours means the starter is weak, and cold or missed feedings are the first things to look at.
4. If you have any of the old flour left, run a second identical jar beside it. That side-by-side is the only thing that actually answers your question about the flour.
