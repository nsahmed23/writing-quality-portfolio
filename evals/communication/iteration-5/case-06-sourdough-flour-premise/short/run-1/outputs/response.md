A new flour brand almost certainly did not kill your starter. The yeast and bacteria live in the culture itself; flour is their food, not their source, so a different bag changes how fast they eat and how well the dough holds the gas they make. Both of those read as "it stopped rising," and neither one is death.

I can't see your jar, the two bags, or your kitchen thermometer, so I can't tell you which of these you hit. Here is what actually changes, then how to identify it in about a day.

**What a brand switch changes**

- **Gluten strength.** All-purpose flours are not interchangeable: roughly 9% protein for a soft-wheat brand like White Lily, ~10.5% for Gold Medal, ~11.7% for King Arthur. Fermentation can be perfect while dough that weak simply cannot hold gas, so it spreads sideways instead of rising up. Flat loaf, healthy starter.
- **Water absorption.** Lower-protein flour takes up less water, so your usual hydration now makes a slacker dough that pancakes during proofing. Same symptom, same innocent starter.
- **Bran and minerals.** If the old bag was whole wheat or rye and the new one is white, you removed most of the minerals, enzymes, and bran-borne microbes. That is a real slowdown, and it shows up gradually over several feedings rather than overnight.
- **Malted barley.** Most conventional US all-purpose flour includes a little malted barley flour, which supplies amylase, the enzyme that breaks starch into sugars the yeast can actually eat. Many organic and European-style flours are unmalted. Unmalted means slower sugar release, which means a slower, shallower rise.
- **Bleaching and heat treatment.** Bleached flour ferments somewhat slower than unbleached. Heat-treated flour (labeled "heat-treated" or "safe to eat raw") has denatured enzymes and weakened gluten, and that one genuinely drags.

What does kill a starter: sustained heat above roughly 120F, real mold (fuzzy colored spots, not the gray liquid), a salt-heavy feed, or weeks of neglect at room temperature. Gray liquid on top and a nail-polish or acetone smell mean hungry, not dead. Worth naming the other suspect too: it is September, and a kitchen that slid from 78F to 68F can easily double your timings with no flour involved at all.

**How to tell which one it is**

1. **Read both bags side by side.** Check protein grams per serving, bleached versus unbleached, whether "malted barley flour" appears in the ingredients, any heat-treatment language, and white versus whole grain. Protein percent is grams divided by serving grams, so 3g per 30g is about 10%. Labels round to the nearest gram, so this is coarse, but it is plenty to spot a real gap.
2. **Test the starter alone, away from any dough.** Feed 20g starter with 50g new flour and 50g water, mark the level with a rubber band, and keep it somewhere 76 to 80F. A healthy culture roughly doubles, domes on top, and smells tangy. If it does that, the starter is fine and your problem is dough strength or proof timing, not the microbes.
3. **Run the two flours head to head** if you have any of the old bag left: two identical jars, same room, same ratio, same hour. The difference in time-to-peak isolates the flour from everything else.
4. **Measure the actual proofing spot** with a thermometer rather than estimating. Compare it to what your kitchen was doing last month.

If step 2 doubles and the loaf still spreads, the flour is weaker than your recipe expects: cut water by 5 to 10% of the flour weight, add a fold or two during bulk, and blend in bread flour or shorten bulk fermentation. If step 2 is sluggish instead, add 10 to 20% whole rye or whole wheat to your feedings, feed twice daily at 1:5:5, and hold it near 78F for three days before judging it.
