Flour brands do not kill sourdough starters. A starter is a mixed population of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria, and ending it takes real violence: heat above roughly 120F, a heavy dose of salt, or weeks of neglect until mold takes hold. A different bag of flour is not in that category. What a flour switch genuinely does is change how fast the culture ferments and how well the dough holds the gas it produces, and both of those look exactly like "stopped rising." So the timing you noticed is probably real. The mechanism is a slowdown, not a die-off.

One thing I cannot tell from here, and it splits the diagnosis: is the jar of starter itself failing to dome and double, or is the starter lively while the loaves come out flat? A flat jar is a fermentation problem. A lively jar with flat loaves is a gluten or shaping problem, and the fixes differ.

Likely causes, roughly in order:

- **Protein content.** Bread flour runs 12 to 14 percent protein, all-purpose 10 to 11.5. If you dropped down, the culture may be fermenting fine while the weaker gluten network cannot trap the gas, so the dough spreads instead of rising. The nutrition panel gives you this: grams of protein per serving divided by the serving size.
- **Measuring by volume.** Brands differ in density, so a cup of the new flour can be meaningfully more or less flour than a cup of the old. That silently shifts both your hydration and your feeding ratio. Moving to a scale resolves a surprising share of post-switch "dead starter" reports.
- **Malt.** Many US flours carry added diastatic malt, an enzyme that frees sugar for the yeast early. Unmalted and many organic flours do not, so fermentation starts slower and peaks later.
- **Bleached or heat-treated flour.** Bleached all-purpose still works, just less vigorously. Flour labeled heat-treated or "safe to eat raw" is the one case where the flour itself suppresses activity, because the heat damages starch and enzymes. Even that slows the culture rather than sterilizing it.
- **Something else that changed the same week.** Kitchen temperature dominates everything else; fermentation roughly halves in speed for every 15F drop. Correlation with the flour switch is suggestive, not proof.

To isolate it in a day:

1. Set up two small jars, each with 20g starter, 20g water, 20g flour. Use the old flour in one if you have any left, the new flour in the other. Same water, same shelf.
2. Mark the starting level with tape or a rubber band. Check at 4, 8, and 12 hours, noting both peak height and when the peak arrives.
3. If the new-flour jar clearly lags, the flour is your variable. Add 20 to 25 percent whole wheat or rye to every feeding, which supplies minerals and enzymes that white flour is stripped of. If both jars lag, it is temperature or the state of the culture, and the brand is a red herring.

To rebuild a sluggish starter: feed 1:1:1 by weight twice a day at 75 to 80F for two or three days. Use filtered water, or tap water left out overnight, if yours is chlorinated. No sugar and no commercial yeast; both mask the problem rather than fix it.

Telling dormant from actually gone: a sharp vinegar or nail-polish smell means hungry, not dead. Grey liquid on top is alcohol, harmless, so stir it in or pour it off. Fuzzy growth in pink, orange, or black is mold, and that is the only case that calls for discarding and starting over.
