# Competing motivations and exceptions

- New-first order can create contrast, surprise, correction, or a deliberate scene/topic shift.
- Chronological order can outrank end emphasis when sequence itself is evidence or instruction.
- Passive can preserve the affected entity/topic, suppress an unknown/irrelevant agent, or fit scientific register.
- A nominalization can maintain a topic string: “The committee decided… This decision…”
- Repetition can be the clearest backward link; avoid thesaurus variation that severs a lexical chain.
- Heavy-first order can be required by a condition, legal scope, safety warning, or periodic design.
- Multiple stress positions can be deliberately controlled with colon, semicolon, dash, or separate sentences.
- Pronouns are not automatically cohesive: use a noun when two antecedents compete.
- Transition words are warranted when they state a real relation not otherwise obvious.
