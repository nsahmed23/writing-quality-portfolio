# Original adjudication examples

These examples demonstrate output shape; they are not substitutes for a dated live evidence check.

## General professional prose

**Question:** “Is *different than* wrong in a formal US report?”  
**Status:** context dependent; *different from* is the low-risk comparison form, while *different than* can prevent an awkward clause (*the outcome was different than we expected*).  
**Action:** inspect the exact complement, verify Garner’s entry before reporting an LCI stage, and recast only if the alternative stays natural.

## Technical documentation

**Question:** “Can an SDK guide say *data is cached*?”  
**Status:** standard in computing when *data* is construed as a mass aggregate; some scientific fields retain plural *data are*.  
**Recommendation:** follow the product glossary/house style and keep agreement consistent; do not call the competing field convention ungrammatical.

## API documentation

**Question:** “The request body *is comprised of* three fields—keep it?”  
**Status:** contested enough to distract some formal readers.  
**Recommendation:** write “The request body contains three fields.” The recast is shorter and avoids adjudicating the disputed construction; do not invent a Garner stage.

## Security or architecture writing

**Question:** “Should the policy say *whomever the gateway authenticates*?”  
**Diagnosis:** case is controlled inside the embedded clause: the gateway authenticates **whomever**, but the whole clause may occupy another role.  
**Recommendation:** retain *whomever* in a highly formal policy if the construction remains readable, or recast as “any user the gateway authenticates.”

## Executive memo

**Question:** “The pilot produced *less incidents*.”  
**Status:** in a formal US memo, *fewer incidents* is the conventional low-risk form for a count plural.  
**Revision:** “The pilot produced fewer incidents.” Preserve the pilot’s measured range and uncertainty; this is not permission to strengthen the result.

## Research report

**Question:** “Is sentence-initial *however* an error?”  
**Status:** standard when punctuation and discourse relation are correct; a ban is a style folklore rule, not grammar.  
**Recommendation:** keep it if it accurately marks contrast and does not become a mechanical transition.

## Scientific or analytical prose

**Question:** “Every participant uploaded *their* sample log.”  
**Status:** singular *they* is standard for indefinite reference and takes plural-form agreement.  
**Recommendation:** retain unless a governing publication policy requires a different construction; never infer Garner’s uninspected fifth-edition classification.

## Email

**Question:** “Who should I send this to?”  
**Status:** standard neutral/informal email. *To whom should I send this?* is more formal, not more grammatical in every setting.  
**Recommendation:** match the relationship and voice; do not introduce stiffness merely to display case marking.

## Clean prose—no change

“Each administrator must rotate their key before Friday.” In a contemporary general workplace context, the pronoun is clear, inclusive, and standard; leave it alone unless a named house rule or referent requires another form.

## Dialect and quotation boundary

A transcript records “They might could finish tonight.” Preserve the quotation. In editorial commentary, identify it as a regional multiple-modal construction if relevant; do not call its speakers illogical. Translate to the target edited standard only when paraphrasing and label the paraphrase.

## Unresolved change

A new construction appears in a small 2026 web sample but lacks reliable edited-corpus, dictionary, and field evidence. Return `emerging; unresolved for formal edited use`, provide the query limitations and retrieval date, and offer a neutral recast. Frequency in raw search results is not a formal-acceptance verdict.
