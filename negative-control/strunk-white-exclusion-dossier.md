## 1. Executive finding

*The Elements of Style* should remain a read negative control, not become an eighth writing skill.

The 1918 Strunk text contains several sound default checks: repair dangling opening modifiers, preserve logical parallelism, keep dependent words close enough to parse, organize paragraphs around intelligible topics, and use sentence endings deliberately. It also contains important qualifications that later slogans often erase: passive voice can be necessary; fragments can create warranted emphasis; loose sentences can prevent over-formality; “omit needless words” does not mean that every sentence must be short; periodic delay can justify separating related words; and sentence beginnings can also carry emphasis.

Those useful checks already have more precise portfolio owners. Punctuation and disputed convention belong to `usage-adjudicator`; actor–action alignment to `sentence-clarity`; redundancy and framing to `concision`; topic continuity and stress placement to `cohesion-emphasis`; purposeful fragments, parallelism, and branching syntax to `sentence-variety`; and paragraph or document hierarchy to `memo-structure` or `doc-typing`. The remaining Strunk-and-White material consists principally of mnemonic goals without operational tests, register-specific taste, historically dated usage judgments, platform conventions, or grammatical misanalysis documented by Pullum.

An independent skill would therefore create severe trigger collision while reintroducing crude defaults—active at all costs, indiscriminate deletion, modifier aversion, and “standard” as an unconditioned verdict—that the portfolio explicitly rejects.

### Provenance safeguards

1. **Full access is never inferred from a preview.** “Full” above means the complete relevant text was available; “partial” identifies the exact visible layer.
2. **The fourth edition is not represented as fully extracted.** Its publisher-visible headings can be classified, but unseen explanations, qualifications, examples, and headwords cannot be inventoried or page-cited.
3. **Rule wording and source judgment are separated from portfolio disposition.** A source rule may be historically or pedagogically important without being a valid universal diagnostic.
4. **Pullum is used for inspectable grammatical demonstrations, not as an empirical oracle.** His parsing evidence can refute a grammatical classification; his rhetoric about educational damage has a different evidentiary status.
5. **Current-edition claims are date-stamped.** Through the cutoff, the fourth edition remains the latest substantiated substantive textual revision. The 2005 illustrated and 2009 anniversary issues are later presentations of that edition branch, not a demonstrated fifth textual edition.
6. **No copyrighted edition is reconstructed.** This dossier paraphrases judgments and inventories public headings; it does not reproduce fourth-edition entries or White's example corpus.

## 3. Edition history and material changes

### 3.1 The privately printed 1918 text

The 1918 Cornell text has six sections:

1. Introductory
2. Elementary Rules of Usage
3. Elementary Principles of Composition
4. A Few Matters of Form
5. Words and Expressions Commonly Misused
6. Words Commonly Misspelled

It numbers eight usage rules 1–8 and ten composition principles 9–18. Its introduction expressly limits the undertaking: the book covers only a small part of style, and a strong writer may violate a rule when a compensating merit justifies the choice. That qualification is operationally important. Treating the text as a set of absolute prohibitions misrepresents Strunk's own frame.

### 3.2 The 1920 trade edition

The 1920 Harcourt trade edition retains the core method but changes the apparatus:

- Usage has seven numbered rules rather than eight.
- The former line-end word-division rule is no longer rule 8.
- Composition principles are renumbered 8–18.
- The spelling material is reorganized.
- A seventh section, exercises on the usage and composition chapters, is added.

This is a material structural and pedagogical revision, not a wholly different style system. The same central maxims—paragraph unity, active voice, positive form, omission, parallelism, proximity, tense consistency, and emphatic endings—remain.

### 3.3 White's revision and later editions

Cornell's institutional history records this sequence:

- 1918: Strunk's privately printed classroom book.
- 1957: E. B. White discusses the book in *The New Yorker*.
- 1959: Macmillan publishes the Strunk-and-White revision. White modernizes and expands the text and adds the essay-like chapter “An Approach to Style.”
- 1972 and 1979: further revised editions.
- 1999: fourth edition, copyright 2000.
- 2005: Maira Kalman illustrated edition.
- 2009: fiftieth-anniversary presentation.

Pearson describes the fourth-edition revisions as limited. Visible changes include Roger Angell's foreword, a new glossary, and revised pronoun discussion addressing sexist language. Through 2026-08-19, no authoritative fifth substantive textual edition was found.

### 3.4 Why edition identity matters

“Strunk,” “Strunk and White,” and “the current *Elements*” are not interchangeable corpora. Pullum documents, for example, that a restrictive-*which* prohibition associated with White was not Strunk's original rule and that Strunk's own prose contained counterexamples. Likewise, split-infinitive material appears in the trade lineage rather than the original 1918 numbered rules. A portfolio principle must therefore identify the exact edition and authorial layer instead of attributing every later prescription to Strunk.

### 4.1 Section I: Introductory

**Operational content.** Strunk presents a small handbook of frequently violated usage rules and compositional principles, not a comprehensive theory of prose. He allows departures when a writer obtains a compensating advantage.

**Disposition.** **Still useful as a compact reminder** and as a portfolio-wide exception rule: do not convert a default into an absolute; preserve a marked structure when its rhetorical benefit exceeds its cost. The section does not supply an independent diagnostic skill.

### 4.2 Section II: Elementary Rules of Usage

#### Rule 1: Form the possessive singular with apostrophe plus *s*

- **Type:** punctuation/inflectional convention.
- **Useful core:** identify the intended possessive form and apply the audience's current convention consistently.
- **Limitations:** proper names, classical names, institutional house styles, and pronunciation can produce accepted alternatives.
- **Disposition:** **Already covered by `usage-adjudicator`**; current dictionaries and the governing style sheet control.

#### Rule 2: Use a comma after each item in a series of three or more

- **Type:** punctuation convention with possible semantic consequences.
- **Useful core:** use punctuation that exposes list structure and prevents attachment ambiguity.
- **Limitations:** the serial comma is a house-style choice when meaning is unaffected; it is not a grammatical virtue by itself.
- **Disposition:** **Register-specific** and **already covered by `usage-adjudicator`**.

#### Rule 3: Enclose parenthetic expressions with commas

- **Type:** clause/phrase integration and punctuation.
- **Useful core:** distinguish integrated/restrictive material from supplementary/nonrestrictive material, then punctuate accordingly.
- **Limitations:** not every interrupting expression is best delimited by commas; parentheses and dashes encode different relations and strengths.
- **Disposition:** **Superseded by a more precise diagnostic** in `usage-adjudicator` and `sentence-clarity`.

#### Rule 4: Put a comma before a conjunction that introduces an independent clause

- **Type:** punctuation of coordination.
- **Useful core:** make independent-clause boundaries visible where needed.
- **Limitations:** very short, tightly linked clauses may omit the comma under some house styles; punctuation can mark rhetorical pace.
- **Disposition:** **Already covered by `usage-adjudicator`**.

#### Rule 5: Do not join independent clauses with only a comma

- **Type:** sentence-boundary convention.
- **Useful core:** identify a comma splice and choose a period, semicolon, conjunction, or purposeful rhetorical exception.
- **Limitations:** literary dialogue and deliberately paratactic prose sometimes use a comma splice effectively; formal edited prose normally does not.
- **Disposition:** **Register-specific**; adjudicate in `usage-adjudicator`, with `sentence-variety` owning deliberate rhetorical exceptions.

#### Rule 6: Do not break one grammatical sentence into two

- **Type:** fragment diagnosis.
- **Useful core:** determine whether a purported sentence lacks an independent clause or strands a dependent element.
- **Strunk's own qualification:** a fragment may be used for warranted emphasis.
- **Failure of the maxim:** an automatic merger can erase rhythm, emphasis, dialogue realism, interface brevity, or an intentional answer fragment.
- **Disposition:** **Grammatically overbroad if treated as a ban**. `usage-adjudicator` identifies formal completeness; `sentence-variety` decides whether a purposeful fragment serves the passage.

#### Rule 7: Make an initial participial phrase modify the grammatical subject

- **Type:** dangling-modifier/dependency diagnostic.
- **Useful core:** recover the understood subject of the participle and verify that the following subject supplies it.
- **Procedure:** mark the participial opener; ask who performs or experiences its action; compare that referent with the main-clause subject; repair by changing the subject, expanding the opener into a finite clause, or relocating the modifier.
- **Exceptions:** conventional supplements whose understood subject is the speaker or proposition require register-sensitive judgment; do not “repair” a clear conventional construction into worse prose solely by pattern matching.
- **Disposition:** **Still useful**, but **already covered by `sentence-clarity`/`usage-adjudicator`**.

#### Rule 8: Divide words at line endings according to formation and pronunciation

- **Type:** compositor/typesetting operation.
- **Current status:** modern layout engines and house dictionaries control hyphenation; responsive digital layouts often prohibit manual line-end division.
- **Disposition:** **Historically interesting but no longer reliable** and generally **not relevant to this portfolio**.

### 4.3 Section III: Elementary Principles of Composition

#### Rule 9: Make the paragraph the unit of composition; ordinarily one topic per paragraph

- **Type:** paragraph scope and unity.
- **Useful core:** a paragraph should perform an intelligible local job, and an unexplained topic shift is a reader problem.
- **Limitations:** dialogue, lists, definitions, procedural steps, and transitional paragraphs do not fit a literal “one topic” test. A single topic can also require multiple paragraphs because the rhetorical actions differ.
- **Disposition:** **Superseded by** paragraph-purpose and topic-string tests in `cohesion-emphasis`, document-type contracts in `doc-typing`, and support hierarchy in `memo-structure`.

#### Rule 10: Begin a paragraph with a topic sentence and end in conformity with it

- **Type:** paragraph framing and emphasis.
- **Useful core:** readers should be able to infer the paragraph's governing point and understand how its ending completes or advances that point.
- **Limitations:** inductive analysis, narrative, tutorials, incident chronology, and deliberate delayed disclosure may establish the point later. The first sentence may instead link backward or set context.
- **Disposition:** **Register-specific**; `cohesion-emphasis` owns paragraph progression and `memo-structure` owns answer-first argument where appropriate.

#### Rule 11: Prefer active voice

- **Type:** voice, agency, information flow, and responsibility.
- **Useful core:** where agency matters and an active clause provides a clear topic, name the actor and use a specific action verb.
- **Strunk's own qualification:** passive voice is often convenient and sometimes necessary; the paragraph's topic can determine the preferred voice.
- **Counterconditions:** retain passive voice when the patient is the established topic, the agent is unknown or irrelevant, agent suppression is ethically justified and not deceptive, procedure/reporting convention focuses on the affected entity, or active order would break given-before-new flow.
- **Disposition:** **Superseded by more precise diagnostics** in `sentence-clarity` and `cohesion-emphasis`; see Pullum analysis below. Never encode “change every passive to active.”

#### Rule 12: Put statements in positive form

- **Type:** rhetorical framing.
- **Useful core:** when a direct positive assertion expresses the intended claim, it can be easier to process than stacked negation or a vague statement of what is absent.
- **Counterconditions:** negation is semantically indispensable in prohibitions, threat models, safety constraints, falsification, exception handling, contracts, research null results, and correction of a misconception.
- **Disposition:** **Useful but too vague to operationalize alone** and **grammatically overbroad** as a prohibition. Preserve factual polarity.

#### Rule 13: Omit needless words

- **Type:** concision goal.
- **Useful core:** remove words that add no necessary meaning, relation, evidentiary scope, courtesy, rhythm, or voice.
- **Strunk's own qualification:** vigorous prose is concise, but this does not require all sentences to be short or every detail to disappear.
- **Diagnostic defect:** the maxim does not tell an editor how to determine that a word is needless. Pullum correctly treats it as non-procedural unless the decision has already been made.
- **Disposition:** **Superseded** by Williams's redundancy/metadiscourse diagnostics and Lanham's paramedic procedures in `concision`. Preserve justified hedges, legal/scientific scope, citations, definitions, and useful repetition.

#### Rule 14: Avoid a succession of loose sentences

- **Type:** syntactic monotony and information shape.
- **Useful core:** detect a run of clauses that repeatedly state a main assertion and then trail through similarly attached additions.
- **Strunk's own qualification:** loose construction can prevent prose from becoming too formal.
- **Counterconditions:** cumulative detail, conversational momentum, escalating parallelism, and deliberate anaphora may require repeated loose structures.
- **Disposition:** **Already covered by `sentence-variety`**. Diagnose repeated architecture, not merely length; change it only when monotony or hierarchy harms the passage.

#### Rule 15: Express coordinate ideas in similar form

- **Type:** parallelism and logic-to-syntax mapping.
- **Useful core:** when ideas have the same logical status, use structures similar enough that readers can perceive the equivalence.
- **Procedure:** identify the coordinate markers; label the grammatical form of each item; repair mismatches that imply a false hierarchy or obstruct comparison.
- **Limitations:** exact surface repetition is not required, and false symmetry can conceal substantive differences.
- **Disposition:** **Still useful**, but owned by `sentence-variety`, `cohesion-emphasis`, and, for grouped argument, `memo-structure`.

#### Rule 16: Keep related words together

- **Type:** dependency distance and attachment.
- **Useful core:** prevent a modifier, complement, subject, verb, or referent from becoming ambiguous because unrelated material intervenes.
- **Procedure:** identify each modifier's head and each subject–verb or verb–object dependency; move interrupting material or recast the clause if a reader can attach it incorrectly.
- **Strunk's own qualification:** a writer may intentionally delay a related element to create a periodic sentence or suspense.
- **Disposition:** **Still useful**, but owned by `sentence-clarity` and `cohesion-emphasis`; periodic syntax is an explicit `sentence-variety` exception.

#### Rule 17: Keep to one tense in summaries

- **Type:** genre convention and temporal reference.
- **Useful core:** avoid unexplained tense shifts that obscure the sequence or discourse stance.
- **Limitations:** summaries may legitimately distinguish past events, present textual claims, prior evidence, and continuing conditions. Different fields adopt different conventions.
- **Disposition:** **Register-specific** and owned by `usage-adjudicator` plus the governing editorial policy.

#### Rule 18: Place emphatic words at the end

- **Type:** information placement and prosodic/syntactic stress.
- **Useful core:** reserve the clause or sentence ending for material that should receive closure, contrast, or emphasis.
- **Strunk's own qualification:** the beginning can also be emphatic.
- **Limitations:** end position is not automatically the location of the most important proposition; given-before-new flow, end weight, chronology, contrastive fronting, and paragraph continuity interact.
- **Disposition:** **Superseded by** stress-position and information-flow diagnostics in `cohesion-emphasis`.

### 4.4 Section IV: A Few Matters of Form

The 1918 section addresses headings, numerals, parentheses, quotations, references, and titles. These are reproducible conventions, but the controlling authority is the target platform, publication, citation system, or house style—not Strunk's 1918 defaults.

**Disposition:** **Historically interesting** and **already covered by `usage-adjudicator` or platform-specific documentation**. It does not justify a general writing skill.

### 4.5 Section V: Words and Expressions Commonly Misused

This alphabetical section mixes genuine distinctions, grammar, preferred diction, register judgments, and personal taste. Several entries are time-sensitive. The original text rejects or distrusts forms that modern dictionaries and edited corpora now accept, including singular *they* in relevant uses, *nearby*, ordinary plural *people* in numerical contexts, and spellings such as *today*, *tonight*, and *tomorrow* without historical hyphens.

**Disposition:** every entry must be adjudicated afresh by issue, edition, geography, register, and current evidence. The section is a historical input to `usage-adjudicator`, not a binding lookup table and not a reproducible skill by itself.

## 6. White-revised fourth edition: visible chapter and rule inventory

This section accounts only for publisher-visible contents. It does **not** imply full inspection of the fourth-edition prose.

### 6.1 Chapter I: Elementary Rules of Usage

Pearson's visible headings present eleven rules:

1. Singular possessives with apostrophe plus *s* — current convention; `usage-adjudicator`.
2. Commas in a series — house style/ambiguity; `usage-adjudicator`.
3. Commas around parenthetic expressions — integration and meaning; `usage-adjudicator`/clarity.
4. Comma before a conjunction introducing an independent clause — punctuation; `usage-adjudicator`.
5. No comma-only joining of independent clauses — formal-default boundary rule with rhetorical exceptions.
6. Do not split a sentence into fragments — overbroad without the purposeful-fragment exception.
7. Colon after an independent clause introducing a list, appositive, amplification, or quotation — punctuation/convention.
8. Dash for an abrupt break/interruption or an amplifying summary/appositive — rhetorical punctuation, register-sensitive.
9. Subject number determines verb number — grammar, including coordination and notional-agreement complications.
10. Use the proper pronoun case — grammar/usage; current construction-specific evidence controls.
11. Initial participial phrase must refer to grammatical subject — dangling-modifier diagnostic.

All eleven belong to `usage-adjudicator` or `sentence-clarity`; none supplies an independent fault class for an Elements skill.

### 6.2 Chapter II: Elementary Principles of Composition

The visible fourth-edition sequence continues:

12. Choose a suitable design and hold to it — `doc-typing`/`memo-structure`; “suitable” requires genre and reader-task diagnostics.
13. Make the paragraph the unit of composition — `cohesion-emphasis`; not literal one-topic enforcement.
14. Use active voice — conditional actor/topic test, not categorical conversion.
15. Put statements in positive form — preserve semantically necessary negation.
16. Use definite, specific, concrete language — useful audience/evidence heuristic but not universal; abstractions and technical categories can be exact.
17. Omit needless words — `concision`, using explicit padding/redundancy tests.
18. Avoid a succession of loose sentences — `sentence-variety`, with purposeful cumulative syntax protected.
19. Express coordinate ideas in similar form — parallelism test.
20. Keep related words together — dependency/proximity test with periodic exception.
21. Keep one tense in summary — genre- and field-specific convention.
22. Put emphatic words at the end — `cohesion-emphasis`, balanced against topic position and chronology.

Rule 16 is a material addition to the visible sequence relative to the 1918 numbered list. It is valuable as a reminder to prefer evidence-bearing nouns and verbs over empty generality, but “concrete” is not synonymous with “accurate.” *Authentication*, *jurisdiction*, *inflation*, and *uncertainty* are abstractions that may be the exact established concepts a document must discuss.

### 6.5 Chapter V: “An Approach to Style” — all twenty-one visible reminders

The headings below are visible in Pearson's table of contents. The descriptions and portfolio dispositions are derived analysis, not paraphrases of unseen chapter prose.

#### 1. Place yourself in the background

- **Value:** can keep expository attention on subject matter rather than author performance.
- **Failure modes:** unsuitable for personal essays, first-person methods statements, accountability, reflective writing, or recommendations requiring a named judgment.
- **Disposition:** **Register-specific / matter of taste**; no independent diagnostic.

#### 2. Write in a way that comes naturally

- **Value:** protects voice and discourages strained imitation.
- **Failure modes:** “natural” is unobservable and can preserve habitually unclear prose.
- **Disposition:** **Still useful as a compact reminder**, implemented by the portfolio's voice-preservation rule, not as an editing operation.

#### 3. Work from a suitable design

- **Value:** document architecture should fit purpose and reader task.
- **Failure modes:** “suitable” is undefined without genre diagnostics.
- **Disposition:** **Superseded by** `doc-typing` and `memo-structure`.

#### 4. Write with nouns and verbs

- **Value:** strong subjects and specific verbs often carry more information than empty modification.
- **Failure modes:** adjectives and adverbs can encode essential degree, scope, time, manner, or risk; nominal terms may be exact topics.
- **Disposition:** **Superseded by** character/action and concision diagnostics; **grammatically overbroad** as a modifier ban.

#### 5. Revise and rewrite

- **Value:** sound process advice.
- **Failure modes:** no trigger, diagnosis, or stopping condition; can encourage needless churn.
- **Disposition:** **Useful but too vague to operationalize alone**. Portfolio rule: leave clean material alone.

#### 6. Do not overwrite

- **Value:** warns against inflated phrasing and disproportionate display.
- **Failure modes:** “overwriting” depends on genre, voice, and rhetorical purpose.
- **Disposition:** `concision` plus register preservation; **matter of taste** at the boundary.

#### 7. Do not overstate

- **Value:** protects evidentiary scope.
- **Failure modes:** underclaiming can also mislead; strong conclusions may be fully supported.
- **Disposition:** portfolio-wide semantic-preservation rule; `usage-adjudicator` or subject-matter review handles modal force.

#### 8. Avoid qualifiers

- **Value:** meaningless intensifiers and reflexive hedging can dilute claims.
- **Failure modes:** justified uncertainty, evidentiary limits, legal conditions, scientific probability, and security assumptions are meaning, not clutter.
- **Disposition:** **Grammatically/rhetorically overbroad** as a ban. `concision` may remove only a qualifier that does no semantic work.

#### 9. Do not affect a breezy manner

- **Value:** discourages forced informality.
- **Failure modes:** conversational tone may be correct for email, onboarding, tutorials, or product guidance.
- **Disposition:** **Register-specific / matter of taste**.

#### 10. Use orthodox spelling

- **Value:** audience expectations and consistency matter.
- **Failure modes:** “orthodox” varies by national variety, dialect, brand, technical field, identity, and time.
- **Disposition:** `usage-adjudicator`, with geography and house style explicit.

#### 11. Do not explain too much

- **Value:** remove explanation that repeats what the intended reader already knows or obscures the task.
- **Failure modes:** tutorials, safety material, accessibility support, and novice documentation often require redundancy and scaffolding.
- **Disposition:** `doc-typing` determines the reader's knowledge state; `concision` cuts only genuine repetition.

#### 12. Do not construct awkward adverbs

- **Value:** flags manner formations that sound strained or obscure.
- **Failure modes:** “awkward” is taste unless supported by current usage evidence; productive morphology permits new forms.
- **Disposition:** `usage-adjudicator`; **too vague alone**.

#### 13. Make sure the reader knows who is speaking

- **Value:** protects referential continuity and dialogue attribution.
- **Failure modes:** deliberate ambiguity or close dialogue can make repeated tags intrusive.
- **Disposition:** `cohesion-emphasis` for referents; `sentence-variety` protects deliberate omission.

#### 14. Avoid fancy words

- **Value:** avoid prestige diction that makes a familiar idea harder for the audience.
- **Failure modes:** “fancy” can stigmatize exact technical terms, legitimate dialect, and authorial voice.
- **Disposition:** **Matter of taste / register-specific**; use audience comprehension and term-of-art tests instead.

#### 15. Do not use dialect unless your ear is good

- **Value:** warns against unconvincing or caricatured representation.
- **Failure modes:** “good ear” is subjective; representation also raises consent, identity, stereotyping, and audience questions.
- **Disposition:** **Genre- and ethics-specific**, outside the core portfolio.

#### 16. Be clear

- **Value:** legitimate goal.
- **Failure modes:** it names no reader problem, diagnostic signal, or revision operation.
- **Disposition:** **Useful but too vague to operationalize**; the entire portfolio supplies the missing diagnostics.

#### 17. Do not inject opinion

- **Value:** distinguishes evidence from unsupported editorializing in genres that require neutrality.
- **Failure modes:** recommendations, arguments, reviews, decision memos, and expert assessments require explicit judgment.
- **Disposition:** **Register- and genre-specific**; false as a universal rule.

#### 18. Use figures of speech sparingly

- **Value:** excessive or mixed figurative language can obscure technical claims.
- **Failure modes:** there is no reproducible threshold; analogy and metaphor can be explanatory tools.
- **Disposition:** **Matter of taste / genre-specific**; no independent operation.

#### 19. Do not take shortcuts at the cost of clarity

- **Value:** good stop condition: compression must not erase relations, definitions, scope, or prerequisites.
- **Failure modes:** it does not define either shortcut or clarity.
- **Disposition:** **Still useful as a compact reminder**, operationalized in all skills' meaning-preservation tests.

#### 20. Avoid foreign languages

- **Value:** unexplained code-switching can exclude an intended audience.
- **Failure modes:** borrowed terms, names, quotations, legal phrases, internationalization examples, and bilingual communication may be necessary and exact.
- **Disposition:** **Historically and culturally unreliable as a blanket rule**; use audience/context and definition tests.

#### 21. Prefer the standard to the offbeat

- **Value:** conventional forms can reduce reader effort in formal contexts.
- **Failure modes:** “standard” varies by dialect, geography, field, genre, and time; the rule can erase voice and legitimate innovation.
- **Disposition:** `usage-adjudicator`, conditioned on audience and register; **matter of taste** when no comprehension or convention problem exists.

## 7. Specific maxims under test

### “Omit needless words”

Retain only as a memorable label for a procedure supplied elsewhere. A reproducible `concision` pass must identify observable padding—empty framing, repeated proposition, redundant modifier, weak verb plus action noun, needless metadiscourse—then test whether deletion changes scope, politeness, rhythm, legal force, scientific caution, or technical precision. The maxim alone cannot perform those decisions.

### “Use the active voice”

Retain only as a conditional agency prompt: “Would naming the actor here improve responsibility or comprehension without damaging topic flow?” It must never override a passive that keeps the established topic first, places new information later, suppresses an irrelevant/unknown agent, or accurately reflects the discourse's focus.

### “Use definite, specific, concrete language”

Retain as an evidence prompt, not an anti-abstraction rule. “Specific” means appropriately bounded for the claim and audience. Abstract nouns may be precise terms of art or established discourse topics. A concrete substitute can be less accurate if it narrows a general proposition without evidence.

### Advice about adverbs and qualifiers

Remove only a modifier that adds no recoverable meaning or falsely strengthens/weakens the evidence. Preserve modifiers encoding time, degree, probability, frequency, manner, jurisdiction, exception, or uncertainty. The presence of an adverb or hedge is not itself a fault signal.

### Paragraph advice

Translate “one topic” into a paragraph-purpose test: What job does the paragraph perform, how does it link backward, and how does it advance the document? Do not require a topic sentence at the opening of every tutorial step, narrative beat, inductive analysis, or transition.

### Usage and grammar advice

Every usage verdict requires edition, register, geography, dialect, field, and current evidence. The historical headword lists are evidence of past prescription, not current adjudications.

### White's “An Approach to Style”

Read as an essay of preferences and reminders. Its useful values—natural voice, revision, evidentiary restraint, clarity, proportion—become portfolio stop conditions. Its prescriptions about modifiers, dialect, opinion, foreign words, and “standard” language require genre and sociolinguistic qualification. The chapter does not present an executable diagnostic system.

### The lecture anecdote

White's introduction famously depicts Strunk filling class time by repeating terse rules, especially the omission maxim. Because the fourth-edition introduction was not fully inspectable here, this dossier does not reproduce or page-cite its wording. Operationally, the anecdote explains memorability and pedagogical theater; repetition does not convert the maxim into a test for identifying needless language.

## 8. Pullum's grammatical critique

### 8.1 Passive-voice misidentification

Pullum examines the four example pairs offered around the active-voice principle. Only the rooster example actually contrasts a passive clause with an active counterpart. The other three source sentences are not passive constructions. This is not a difference of stylistic taste: English passive voice has identifiable grammatical structure, and labelling nonpassives as passives makes the lesson unreliable.

The resulting portfolio safeguard is strict:

1. Diagnose passive voice grammatically; do not infer it from weak prose, a form of *be*, or an absent actor.
2. Separate voice from agency. An active intransitive clause may omit an agent-like cause; a passive can name its agent in a *by*-phrase.
3. Compare discourse order. If the affected entity is the established topic, a passive may provide better given-before-new flow than the active alternative.
4. Ask whether naming or suppressing an agent changes accountability.
5. Revise only when the chosen voice creates an actual reader or responsibility problem.

Pullum also notes that Strunk and White's surrounding prose acknowledges passive voice can be necessary and topic-dependent. The strongest conclusion is therefore not “the book bans passive” but “its slogan, examples, grammatical labels, and qualifications do not form a reliable executable procedure.”

### 8.2 Split infinitives and stress

Pullum argues that splitting an infinitive is grammatical and historically well attested. He also challenges White's stress account: moving an adverb after the verb can give it stronger end stress, contrary to the claimed rhetorical effect and in tension with the book's own end-emphasis advice.

**Disposition:** exact placement should be decided by ambiguity, naturalness, scope, rhythm, and intended stress—not folklore prohibition.

### 8.3 *None*, initial *however*, and restrictive *which*

Pullum supplies edited-literature evidence for plural agreement with *none*, sentence-initial *however*, and restrictive *which*. He further documents that Strunk used restrictive *which* before White added the later restriction and removed conflicting examples.

**Disposition:** these are `usage-adjudicator` questions. Report authority disagreement, register, and current evidence; do not present a time-sensitive preference as grammar.

### 8.4 Limits of the critique

Pullum's parses and counterexamples directly undermine specific grammatical claims. His broader judgment that the book caused widespread educational harm is a scholarly-critical inference. The dossier adopts the demonstrated grammatical corrections without treating every rhetorical conclusion in the essay as experimentally established.

## 9. Effective self-violation and internal tension

The authors' own effective departures matter because they show that the successful unit is rhetorical function, not surface compliance.

### 9.1 Modifiers and passive voice in White's explanation

Pullum points to a passage immediately following White's preference for nouns and verbs. The explanation itself uses a negative passive, several adjectives, and a separation between related elements. Pullum's point is not that the passage fails; it works. The problem is that its successful grammar contradicts a literal reading of nearby directives.

**Portfolio lesson:** do not delete adjectives/adverbs, eliminate passive voice, or collapse an interruption merely because a detector finds the form. Test what the form contributes.

### 9.2 Passive constructions in White's introductory prose

Pullum identifies unobjectionable passives in White's own introduction. They preserve the relevant discourse topic and omit or background agents that the passage does not need.

**Portfolio lesson:** an effective passive is a negative fixture. A blanket active conversion must fail validation.

### 9.3 Restrictive *which* in Strunk

Strunk used restrictive *which* in prose that readers can understand. White's later categorical preference against it therefore conflicts with the original author's practice.

**Portfolio lesson:** authorial self-use does not automatically prove universal acceptability, but it disproves a claim that the original work consistently embodied the later absolute. Current usage evidence controls.

### 9.4 Fragments, loose sentences, periodic delay, and initial emphasis

These are not merely accidental violations. The 1918 text itself recognizes their effective uses while stating defaults against fragments, successions of loose sentences, separated related words, and nonfinal emphasis.

**Portfolio lesson:** Strunk's qualifications should defeat simplified descendants of his maxims. `sentence-variety` and `cohesion-emphasis` must preserve a marked form that has a clear rhetorical job.

## 11. Negative-control fixtures

These behaviors must be rejected by the portfolio:

1. **Blanket active conversion**  
   Text: “The encryption key was rotated after the incident.”  
   Context: the key is the established topic; the rotating operator is irrelevant and already documented.  
   Required result: retain passive; do not invoke the slogan as controlling authority.

2. **Erasing responsible uncertainty**  
   Text: “The patch probably reduces exposure, but the sample is too small to estimate the effect precisely.”  
   Required result: retain *probably* and the limitation; neither is a needless qualifier.

3. **Destroying a term of art**  
   Text: “Authentication occurs before authorization.”  
   Required result: retain both nominal terms; they name distinct established security concepts.

4. **Forcing a fragment into a full sentence**  
   Text after a risk list: “One exception: emergency revocation.”  
   Required result: retain if the fragment provides deliberate warning emphasis and fits the register.

5. **Mechanical modifier deletion**  
   Text: “The service retries failed requests twice.”  
   Required result: retain *failed* and *twice*; they specify eligibility and count.

6. **Serial-comma absolutism**  
   Required result: follow the target house style unless ambiguity demands intervention; do not call the alternative ungrammatical.

7. **Topic-sentence absolutism**  
   Context: a tutorial lets the learner perform an action before explaining why it worked.  
   Required result: do not impose answer-first memo form or an abstract opening thesis.

8. **Standard-language absolutism**  
   Context: informal dialogue or audience-appropriate regional form.  
   Required result: explain register and audience; do not label legitimate variation simply wrong.

9. **Restrictive-*which* folklore**  
   Required result: distinguish house-style preference from grammatical acceptability and report current evidence.

10. **Variety for its own sake**  
    Context: deliberate parallel commands in an incident runbook.  
    Required result: retain repetition because it supports scanning and operational consistency.

## 13. Why no independent skill should be built

An executable skill requires a distinct fault class, observable signals, a diagnostic sequence, a revision procedure, exceptions, stop conditions, semantic-preservation tests, and non-colliding triggers. *The Elements of Style* does not supply a distinct combination of those components.

- Its strongest mechanics are ordinary usage questions.
- Its strongest sentence diagnostics are already owned by clarity, cohesion, concision, or syntax skills with explicit exceptions.
- Its memorable maxims usually identify desired outcomes rather than how to diagnose a failure.
- Its White-era style reminders are often genre preferences.
- Its usage list changes with time and cannot function without current evidence.
- Its passive discussion includes grammatical misclassification.
- Its own successful prose demonstrates why surface-form prohibitions fail.

Building an `elements-of-style` skill would also make routing worse. A request to “clarify,” “tighten,” “fix flow,” “vary these sentences,” “structure this memo,” or “which form is accepted?” would all plausibly activate it, duplicating every portfolio pass and creating contradictory ownership. The negative-control role is more valuable: use the work to test whether the portfolio can preserve a legitimate passive, nominalization, hedge, fragment, repetition, abstraction, dialect form, or syntactic expansion against a famous but less precise maxim.

**Final disposition:** read historically; cite cautiously; retain qualified reminders only where a stronger source supplies an operational method; create no eighth skill.
